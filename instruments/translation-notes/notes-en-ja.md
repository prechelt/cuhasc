# Translation notes: English → Japanese (en → ja)

**Source language:** English
**Target language:** Japanese (日本語)

## Target-language characterization

- **Register / variety.** Japanese sharply separates written-standard from spoken
  registers (である/だ vs. です/ます). Attitude-scale items in Japanese scientific
  questionnaires conventionally use the plain written declarative (…である / …ではない,
  …べきである). I used that throughout, so wording is stable across the polite/plain
  divide respondents might otherwise read into it.
- **Honorifics / status.** Japanese grammar can force status marking (keigo) that
  English leaves unmarked — a real risk for the PO (Power Distance) items, whose whole
  point is neutral description of hierarchy. I deliberately avoided honorific verb forms
  and used the neutral pair 地位の高い人 / 地位の低い人 ("people of higher / lower
  standing") uniformly, so the items describe hierarchy without performing deference.
- **Derivational morphology / UNI.** Kanji compounds share stems easily, which makes
  UNI easy to honor but also easy to forge accidentally. See the CO "welfare" note below.
- **Polysemy / register layering.** Several natural words carry a classical or
  philosophical second sense English lacks (直観, 忠誠); noted where it mattered (MA2).
- **Gender.** The MA items are explicitly about men vs. women, so gender-neutrality does
  not apply; I used the formal, symmetrical pair 男性 / 女性.

## Most difficult decisions

### 1. MA2 — "intuition": 直感 vs. 直観 (AMB)
Japanese has two homophones. **直観 (A)** is the philosophical/classical term ("immediate
intellectual apprehension", Kantian *Anschauung*); **直感 (B)** is the everyday
"gut feeling". English "intuition" here is the ordinary, everyday sense contrasted with
"logical analysis". 直観 would inject a scholarly secondary sense absent from the source,
so by **AMB/LAM** I chose **直感** — the plain, everyday reading that mirrors the source.

### 2. MA3 — "forcible approach": 力強い vs. 強引 (CON)
"an active, forcible approach" pairs with "active" and reads as *forceful / vigorous* —
mildly positive, and framed as a stereotypically male virtue. **強引 (B)** means
"pushy / coercive / high-handed" and is distinctly negative; **力強い (A)** means
"forceful / powerful" and is neutral-to-positive. By **CON** (match the direction and
strength of connotation) I chose **力強い**, which keeps the source's positive-leaning
valence rather than amplifying it into something pejorative.

### 3. CO "welfare": 福利 vs. 福祉 vs. 利益 (UNI + disambiguation)
"welfare (of the group)" appears in CO3 and CO5 and had to be rendered uniformly (**UNI**).
Three candidates: **福祉** reads as institutional "social welfare / services" (wrong
frame); **利益** ("interest / benefit") was already spent on CO1's "self-interest"
(自己の利益), so reusing it would blur the individual-vs-group contrast the CO group is
built on; **福利** ("well-being / benefit") is neutral, collective, and kept distinct from
CO1's 利益. I used **福利** in both CO3 and CO5.

## Minor notes

- **Register:** the whole instrument uses the plain written declarative (…である,
  …べきである) standard for Japanese questionnaires.
- **LT phrasing:** noun-phrase items (LT1, LT3, LT4) stay bare phrases; gerund items
  (LT2, LT5, LT6) are nominalized with …こと, preserving the source's phrase-vs-clause
  form. Parenthetical glosses translated: 倹約 (Thrift), 粘り強さ (Persistence).
- **UNI honored** for 指示 ("instructions", UN1/UN2/UN5), 手順 ("procedures", UN2/UN4),
  集団 ("group", CO group), and 将来の成功のために ("for success in the future", LT5/LT6).
- **Scales / dimensions:** used the established Japanese Hofstede terms (権力格差,
  不確実性の回避, 集団主義, 長期志向, 男性性) and conventional Likert endpoints
  (全くそう思わない … 非常にそう思う; 非常に重要でない … 非常に重要である).

Overall the translation was largely straightforward; Japanese matches the source items
well, and the only genuinely delicate choices were the three connotation/ambiguity
calls above.
