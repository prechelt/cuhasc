---
description: Check whether an article provides evidence that is usable for the handbook
---
We aim at creating a handbook that helps software teams cope with culture-induced difficulties
with their Scrum process.

The idea is that cultural properties of people as described by the Hofstede cultural dimensions theory
(Power distance, Individualism vs. Collectivism, Uncertainty avoidance,
Motivation towards Achievement and Success/Masculinity, Long-term orientation)
can get in the way of performing certain activities of Scrum in the intended manner.

For example, Retrospectives cannot help if nobody is willing to talk about what is going wrong,
planning cannot help if nobody is willing to state that something will take long to build,
daily standup cannot help if nobody is talking about the future, and so on.
Whatever the exact relationships may be (the above is not intended as claims of such relationships),
we are looking for articles describing phenomena in Scrum software teams that MIGHT be
created or furthered by cultural properties.

So we are looking for software engineering articles (scientific or high-quality practitioner literature)
with the following properties:
- Talks about the work of a specific Scrum team
- Provides some information (often indirect) about the cultural background of that team,
  for example by stating in which country the team is located
- States a problem (or several) with the execution of any aspect of Scrum
- Describes enough of the mechanism underlying that problem that one may(!) be able to connect it
  to culture-induced behavioral tendencies.

We say such an article contains "Scrum-and-culture information".

Your task is this:
- Given the text of such an article, decide whether it contains, possibly contains,
  or does not contain Scrum-and-culture information.
- In case it does not contain such information, make a short statement how it does not.
  E.g.: "This is a survey. It contains only frequency information and makes no statements about
  individual scrum teams." or 
  "The article claims that problems exist. It also names the problems, e.g. 
  'Daily Scrum meetings usually overrun the alloted time', but does not explain how the problems arise.", etc.
- In case it does contain Scrum-and-culture information, find the paragraph (or two short paragraphs)
  in the article that contains the clearest example of such information and report it verbatim.
- In case it possibly contains Scrum-and-culture information, also report such a paragraph, but
  add a comment why you are skeptical that this information is really useful for our work.
  Use this category sparingly.
- If you report a paragraph, also add the best quote or quotes from the article regarding
  the composition of the team (nationality of team members or location of the team, gender composition,
  personality traits).
- If you report a paragraph and happen to have a clear idea which Hofstede cultural effect may be
  at work in that case, explain this idea and provide a supporting argument.
  If you have no such idea, report the paragraph without such a culture statement.

In our experience, few articles contain Scrum-and-culture information, so the
"does not contain" case will likely be frequent.

The skill call will be followed either by a filename (then read the article from that file)
or lots of text (which is then the article text).
